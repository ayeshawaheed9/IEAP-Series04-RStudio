Question 2: How Does Random Noise Affect Linear Regression?

1. Formulate your expectations

Before performing the experiment, I expect the following:

  • Slope: Since the underlying relationship does not change, I expect the estimated slope to remain close to the original value. However, it may vary slightly because of random noise.
  • Intercept: I also expect the estimated intercept to remain close to the original intercept, although it may not be exactly the same in all three cases.
  • Prediction quality: With a small amount of noise, the regression line should describe the observations quite well. As noise increases, the predictions should become less accurate.
  • Explained variance (R-squared): I expect R-squared to decrease as the noise increases because the linear relationship will explain a smaller proportion of the total variation in the data.

My main expectation is that adding noise should not change the theoretical relationship between x and y, but it can make that relationship more difficult to estimate from the observed data.

2. Implement and investigate

I will use the following linear relationship:

[ y = 10 + 2x ]

The theoretical intercept is therefore 10 and the theoretical slope is 2.

I will generate 100 observations with x values ranging from 0 to 100. Then, I will add three different levels of random noise.

5. Comparing the Regression Results

To make the comparison easier, I will extract the intercept, slope, R-squared and RMSE from each model.

RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error) measures the average size of the prediction errors. A lower RMSE indicates better prediction accuracy.

# Calculate RMSE for each model
rmse <- function(model) {
  sqrt(mean(residuals(model)^2))
}

# Create a comparison table
model_comparison <- map_dfr(seq_along(models), function(i) {

  model <- models[[i]]
  model_summary <- summary(model)

  tibble(
    Noise_Level = names(models)[i],
    Intercept = coef(model)[1],
    Slope = coef(model)[2],
    R_squared = model_summary$r.squared,
    RMSE = rmse(model)
  )

})

9. Improving the Experiment

One limitation of this experiment is that it uses only one generated random noise pattern. The estimated slope and intercept may therefore be influenced by the particular random values selected.

To obtain more reliable conclusions, I think it would be better to repeat the experiment many times for each noise level.

For example, I can generate 500 datasets for each noise level and calculate the average slope, average intercept, R-squared and RMSE. This would help determine whether the results observed in the first experiment are consistent or mainly due to one random sample.

set.seed(456)

n_simulations <- 500

simulation_results <- map_dfr(noise_levels, function(noise_sd) {

  map_dfr(seq_len(n_simulations), function(i) {

    simulated_y <- true_intercept +
      true_slope * x +
      rnorm(length(x), mean = 0, sd = noise_sd)

    simulated_model <- lm(simulated_y ~ x)

    tibble(
      Intercept = coef(simulated_model)[1],
      Slope = coef(simulated_model)[2],
      R_squared = summary(simulated_model)$r.squared,
      RMSE = rmse(simulated_model)
    )

  }) %>%
    mutate(Noise_SD = noise_sd)

})

# Average results for each noise level
simulation_summary <- simulation_results %>%
  group_by(Noise_SD) %>%
  summarise(
    Mean_Intercept = mean(Intercept),
    Mean_Slope = mean(Slope),
    Mean_R_squared = mean(R_squared),
    Mean_RMSE = mean(RMSE),
    SD_Slope = sd(Slope),
    .groups = "drop"
  )

kable(

3. Draw Conclusions

After comparing the numerical results with my initial expectations, I found that most of them were confirmed. However, the repeated simulations helped me understand the effect of noise more clearly.

Were my expectations confirmed?

My expectation that the slope and intercept would remain close to their theoretical values was confirmed. Across the 500 simulations, the average slope remained close to 2, changing only from 2.001 to 1.997 across the lowest and highest noise levels. The average intercept also remained close to 10.

I also expected prediction quality to decrease as noise increased, and the results clearly confirmed this. The average R-squared decreased from 0.993 to 0.792, while RMSE increased from 4.970 to 29.745.

What differences did I observe between the three models?

The main difference was the amount of variation around the regression line.

  • Noise SD = 5: The model performed very well, with an average R-squared of 0.993 and an RMSE of 4.970. The observations remained close to the theoretical relationship.
  • Noise SD = 15: The observations became more scattered. R-squared decreased to 0.938 and RMSE increased to 14.925.
  • Noise SD = 30: This model had the largest amount of noise. R-squared decreased further to 0.792, and RMSE reached 29.745.

Another difference was the variability of the estimated slope. Its standard deviation increased from 0.017 at noise level 5 to 0.104 at noise level 30. This shows that the estimated slope became less stable when more noise was added.

What can I conclude about the impact of random noise?

From this experiment, I conclude that random noise does not necessarily change the underlying linear relationship or systematically shift the average regression estimates away from their theoretical values. However, it makes the estimates less precise and reduces the predictive performance of the model.

An important point I noticed is that the average slope and intercept remained almost unchanged across the simulations, but the prediction errors increased considerably. This shows that estimating the general relationship correctly and predicting individual observations accurately are not the same thing.

Overall, my initial expectations were mostly confirmed. Higher random noise makes the data more difficult to predict, reduces the proportion of variation explained by the regression model and increases the variability of the estimated coefficients.
